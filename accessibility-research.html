Accessibility — the whole journey to our plot

Item 5 · 2026-09-15 · Bourj Hammoud / Entering Beirut

Finding: the site has several mapped approaches, but a continuous, independently usable arrival is not established. The nearest bridge is only one component. Its stairs, the route before it, the landing after it, the pavement beside the plot and the building threshold have to work together. An attractive protected interior cannot compensate for an unusable approach.

This is a desk-based analysis and a design brief, not a current access certification. VERIFY means a condition needs evidence; TBD means information is missing. No access score, wheelchair route, crossing safety rating, measured pedestrian flow or gradient has been invented.

1. What the professor’s instruction means

The course lecture’s page 9 connects contours, slope, drainage and section, and asks the student to distinguish highway movement, local movement and pedestrians while examining landings, stopping and service conflicts. Our technical-integration checklist separately requires a continuous accessible route to public spaces and verification of the applicable authority and requirements. These are design obligations in our project record, not proof of existing conditions. Lecture notes, Technical integration.

In plain language: do not just draw a line ending at the plot. Draw what a person actually has to do along it. A route may involve changing street side, negotiating a kerb, climbing, waiting, turning around a parked vehicle, finding the entrance and opening a door. These actions need space and appropriate levels. A line in plan hides most of them.

For this project, accessibility therefore has three scales:

  1. Urban access: which approach and crossing could serve someone coming from each side?
  2. Street access: can the person continue along the edge, negotiate junctions, rest and reach the plot?
  3. Building access: can the person enter with others, find the destination and use public functions independently?

The first two maps, Connections and People & Needs, identify potential links and audiences. Daily Rhythm asks when their journeys may occur. This study asks whether those journeys are usable, including when the building is only partly open.

Two crossings, not two certified step-free choices

C1, nearest the plot: source way 701135687 is a pedestrian bridge. Its southern approach 270786366 and northern approach 701135688 are tagged highway=steps. This supports marking source-mapped stairs. It does not establish present condition, stair dimensions, handrails, a working lift, absence of all ramps or a complete alternative route.

C2, farther east: 1069243892 combines bridge=yes, layer=1 and crossing=zebra. Its associated approaches 1069243890 and 1069243891 are tagged steps. This is a material interpretation trap: the zebra tag alone must not become a drawn at-grade highway crossing. The new map retains the bridge interpretation and flags the conflicting attributes for verification.

Conclusion from these records: neither crossing can currently be presented as a verified step-free solution. This is narrower—and more defensible—than claiming no accessible crossing exists anywhere.

A road line is not a pedestrian strip

Street 80 275315121, Street 52 components 306996681 and 715531021, and Armenia bridge components 452148075 and 452148076 establish mapped road geometry. Their vehicle-lane, one-way and asphalt tags are not evidence of a clear pedestrian width. In particular, vehicle one-way direction is not a ban on walking in the other direction.

3. What local research adds—and what it cannot add

UN-Habitat’s November 2022 account describes five improved alleyways in Maraach, including lighting and drainage; a resident explains that the drainage work improved her children’s walk to school. This supports treating weather and operation as access questions. It is not evidence that our Street 80 approach floods or lacks lighting. UN-Habitat, Urban October in Beirut.

AUB’s 2016 Nabaa/Sis Street study describes delivery-related blockages and shop displays occupying sidewalks, alongside the social and economic value of street activity. That is historical evidence from a different subarea. The transferable question is how to maintain a usable passage while accommodating everyday work—not an assumption that every local shop is an obstruction or should be cleared. AUB, Rethinking Shared Space, printed pp.13–14.

Site-specific inference to test: our industrial/workshop context and plot-side curve make the relationship between delivery, passing and waiting important. We do not yet know actual vehicle sizes, loading hours, obstructions or operating agreements. A delivery schedule may reduce conflicts, but cannot substitute for adequate space and a route that remains available when someone needs it.

The last few metres may determine the whole journey

At X8, the Street 80 bend, the site boundary and the bridge-side approach are close together in plan. That proximity could help create a recognisable arrival, but it could also concentrate people who are turning, waiting, searching for a door or servicing a building. This is a spatial conflict hypothesis, not crash evidence.

A useful design comparison is therefore not “entrance north or south?” in isolation. It is:

  • Which entrance connects to the more continuous pedestrian edge?
  • Can a visitor pause before the door without blocking another person?
  • Can a person arriving from the other direction reach it without an extra unsafe manoeuvre?
  • Does the service vehicle cross the waiting area?
  • Can the route remain available when the outer public area is open and inner rooms are closed?

Resolve these with the ground plan and a level section, before committing the main entrance or building platform. Do not derive slopes from our two-dimensional source lines.

6. From analysis to our nesting concept

The accessibility study changes nesting from merely “putting a protected space inside another” to a sequence of protection that remains easy to enter and use.

  1. An accessible outer threshold. The first protected space should receive people from the street: a clear arrival, shelter and room to orient themselves. It must not force every visitor to climb to a podium.
  2. A legible shared layer. The next space should make the destination understandable, with a clear route onward and room for companions. Avoid turning enclosure into a maze or hiding the public door behind service access.
  3. Quieter inner rooms. Use position, enclosure and buffering to reduce exposure while retaining an independently usable route. If public functions are upstairs, their access cannot be an afterthought.
  4. A parallel service arrangement. Loading and storage should support the building without consuming its arrival space. The exact service location remains a design test, not a new driveway drawn as approved.
  5. An operational boundary. When only one layer stays open, its entrance, necessary facilities and exit arrangements must still function. The Daily Rhythm map informs this test; it does not prove a 24-hour programme is necessary.

This analysis does not select a final entrance, design a new bridge, certify a public right of way or establish a detailed ramp. It gives criteria by which those design decisions can be compared.

Who can actually change each condition?

Responsibility to establish Potential scope What cannot be promised yet
Project team / client Internal ground levels, entrance, waiting, public circulation, facilities and service organisation Ownership outside the plot; final compliance before authority/code review
Street authority and neighbouring frontages Pavement continuity, kerbs, drainage, driveways and permitted stopping That the student project can move kerbs or remove obstructions independently
Bridge / transport infrastructure authorities Crossings, stair alternatives, operation, boarding arrangements A lift, ramp, traffic restriction or new stop without feasibility and approvals

The design should show these dependencies honestly. If an urban link is unresolved, that limitation belongs in the argument rather than being concealed by a green “accessible route” arrow.

7. How the new map communicates the analysis

The Accessibility tab is separate from the older Access tab and preserves Connections, People, Daily Rhythm and all photo maps. It contains 15 research tags, three shaded inspection windows, 11 source-way components, and four approach views. Every tag separates evidence, interpretation, design consequence and the check still needed.

  • Red S: source-mapped steps, not a claim that the entire neighbourhood is inaccessible.
  • Amber ?: continuity requiring inspection. Dashed road components are not footpath directions.
  • Purple C: sensory, resting and operating conditions to check—not measured comfort.
  • Blue A: arrival and service questions; not new existing stops or loading bays.
  • Teal P: matters for the project design; not a pass/fail accessibility result.
  • Shading: authored study windows, not catchments, ownership or barrier intensity.

“Mapped components only” hides inspection tags and shading. It retains the two decks, four stair ways, road components and plot location, with source links. This deliberately does not certify a connected route.

For each segment record: date/time, side of street, start/end locations, view direction, photograph ID, clear width at the narrowest point, level changes, surface, obstructions and whether they are permanent or temporary. Store observations separately from inferred causes. Record equipment/method and uncertainty for measured gradients; a perspective photograph is not a measuring instrument.

Repeat selected stations during a delivery period and early evening. Log actual conditions rather than assuming the published business schedule equals public accessibility. After-rain checks should only be undertaken from safe pedestrian positions. Do not test a dangerous crossing, enter the carriageway to measure it or ask a disabled participant to attempt an unverified route. Engage users about their experiences and preferences rather than simulate disability as evidence.

Completion criteria: a route is ready to support an accessibility claim only when its complete sequence, including thresholds and operation, has evidence. A partial photograph or mapped street is still valuable, but remains a partial check. Until then, present candidate arrivals and unresolved links—not a definitive accessible-route map.

9. What to say in the critique

“Our first map showed that connections exist. This analysis shows why connection alone is not enough. The closest bridge has steps recorded at both ends; the alternative crossing is not established as step-free either. On the same side of the highway, we still need to resolve pavement continuity, the plot-side bend and the entrance level. So the design begins with an accessible, sheltered threshold. Nesting then protects people progressively, without making the protected centre difficult to enter. The map distinguishes what the source shows, what we must measure and what the project can actually control.”
