Protection — selective shelter on an exposed urban plot

The plot needs protection, but protection is not one condition and cannot be demonstrated by one enclosing shape. The corrected plan places it north of the main highway, beside Street 80 and east of the river corridor. These are real spatial relationships. They support investigating exposure; they do not establish a measured noise level, polluted-air boundary, flood depth or absence of shade. The useful architectural question is: which exposure should each layer reduce, for whom, at what height and at what time?

This is the sixth investigation in the agreed urban-to-plot analysis sequence. It follows connections, potential users, daily rhythms and whole-journey accessibility. It is a desk-based research and design brief: VERIFY marks a claim needing confirmation; TBD marks missing information. The new Protection map separates six investigations, includes coloured source corridors and tagged study windows, and keeps measured performance explicitly unknown. Analysis completed on 2026-09-15; the underlying OSM extract is dated 2026-09-13.

1. Read our plan before selecting a protective form

The studio’s environmental analysis asks for sun, wind, hot-season comfort, sea influence, dust, glare, noise and comfortable thresholds. These are related but not interchangeable. A wall is a geometric device; protection is a performance that must be tested at an occupied place. The course question therefore becomes a sequence from source, through the edge, to the person or activity affected. Course lecture, environmental section.

On the corrected map, the main highway lies immediately to the southern side of the site’s local context. Street 80 turns along its eastern and southern interface. Sea Road is farther north, rather than being the same road as the southern highway. The river is west of the site, with road infrastructure and intervening space. Buildings exist around the plot, but their outlines alone do not establish their heights, canopy shade, opening positions or current activity. These distinctions matter: neither “everything is highway” nor “the back is protected” is an adequate reading. Source highway way, Street 80, Sea Road.

The plot has several different interfaces:

  • South: investigate traffic sound, air, arrival from the pedestrian bridge and elevated views together. Do not solve sound by obstructing the arrival.
  • East / Street 80: investigate the last part of the walk, stopping, servicing and entrance shelter. A local road is not automatically a clean or safe edge.
  • West: test afternoon sun and wind-direction cases while retaining the river as regional context, not claiming it provides cooling or flood protection.
  • North: inspect neighbouring massing and the northern road contribution. It is not automatically a quiet, shaded rear.
  • Above: investigate roofs and upper rooms separately. Ground-level shielding is not a transferable roof condition.
  • Below: verify ground, water and drainage conditions before committing to excavation, infiltration or growing systems.

These are authored interpretations of the preserved plan, not verified exposure rankings. The large rectangles on the map are investigation windows, not flood extents, pollutant contours, sound bands or proposed buildings. Small tag positions are discussion anchors. A label can move for readability; its dotted leader is not a route or environmental vector.

2. Noise: locate the listener, not just the road

The southern highway is an obvious corridor to investigate, but the important missing information is the relationship between a sound source and a receiver: a person sitting in a court, someone in an upper quiet room, or people on the roof. Source level, traffic composition, road elevation, building heights and openings are TBD. The northern road and local activity also need comparison before deciding which way a court should open.

Acoustic barriers depend on their height, length, openings and the source–receiver geometry. Upper receivers and gaps can limit their usefulness. Ordinary planting should not simply be credited as equivalent to a solid acoustic barrier. These are general mechanisms, not a promised reduction on our plot. FHWA analysis and abatement guidance.

Design implication for this site: compare a road-side buffer with an offset entry and a less enclosed alternative. Put the same ground and upper receivers into each section. A successful ground court does not justify placing the quietest room directly above it without another test. Service spaces can be considered as part of a buffer, but the entrance must remain identifiable, inclusive and workable rather than becoming the leftover gap in a wall.

The first acoustic drawing should be a section across highway–plot–neighbour, with actual levels marked where known and TBD elsewhere. A second section should test the bridge and proposed upper levels. A plan of measured sound can follow fieldwork; a red distance gradient now would imply evidence we do not have. Map stations N1, N2, C1 and E1 structure this comparison.

3. Air, dust and odour: quieter does not mean cleaner

Traffic is a plausible emission source, but mapped road classification does not establish concentration. Nearby generators, exhaust outlets, workshop emissions, idling and dust-generating activities require their own dated inventory. A2 does not identify a generator; it asks where actual sources and future project intakes/exhausts should be investigated. Use and storey labels from the photo survey cannot substitute for that inventory.

Near-road barriers can alter dispersion rather than simply remove pollution. Their effects depend on geometry and conditions; reductions in one position can coexist with increases near other parts of a barrier. Filtration and building siting are also relevant, but cannot establish outdoor safety. US EPA near-road FAQ, pp. 3–4.

Beirut-specific research reinforces the need to examine urban geometry, weather and source assumptions: Al Aawar and colleagues modelled diesel-generator pollution with building and atmospheric information. This is a city modelling study, not measurements at our plot; its results cannot be used to draw a local plume here. Al Aawar et al., 2021 preprint.

Design implication: compare candidate places for longer stays and incoming air, not merely the appearance of a courtyard. Keep intake, exhaust and loading decisions coordinated in three dimensions. An intake above a wall or roof is not automatically beyond a plume. A future kitchen, workshop or mechanical system can also affect the court and neighbours, so the project must not create the exposure it seeks to avoid.

For nesting, this means distinguishing a shared space open to outside conditions from inner rooms whose ventilation can be managed appropriately. It does not mean all rooms need permanent closure or mechanical ventilation; that decision requires use, climate, air-quality and system studies. Field comparisons should pair source activity, weather and suitable monitoring. Smell and visible dust are useful observations but do not measure every pollutant. A1, A2, W1, W2 and E1 identify the related tests.

4. Sun and heat: make the occupied place comfortable

The new map offers an idealised solar-direction comparison at the site latitude: summer solstice, equinox and winter solstice, each at 09:00, 12:00 and 15:00 solar time. Solar time is not Lebanese clock time. Fixed declinations of +23.44°, 0° and −23.44° are used with latitude 33.89653° and hour angle 15° per hour from solar noon. The altitude follows standard solar geometry; the drawn ray points toward the sun. It is not a cast shadow, radiation estimate or thermal-comfort result. NOAA solar equations.

The resulting idealised noon altitudes are about 79.5° in summer, 56.1° at equinox and 32.7° in winter. These calculated differences explain why one fixed shading move should not be judged using one noon image. Morning and afternoon sun directions also change. The model does not include actual cloud, obstructions, materials or atmospheric refraction; it does not implement a full date/time-zone ephemeris.

Site implication: investigate the western edge at afternoon occupancy, while testing roof shade and useful winter light separately. A canopy over a walkway, a screen beside low-angle sun, a tree and a covered shared court solve different parts of the problem. Place each test where people would actually wait, work, sit or circulate—not only on a roof diagram.

Trees can provide shade and evapotranspirative cooling, but their actual canopy, establishment, water supply and maintenance matter. A green polygon is not a canopy model and supplies no site cooling number. US EPA, benefits of trees and vegetation.

The critical missing model is corrected surrounding heights, including irregular roofs and relevant canopies. Shade should be checked at occupied height, alongside hot surrounding surfaces and air movement. The day-one scheme should not rely entirely on mature trees. Growing areas may need light that seating does not; those requirements should be separated rather than calling the entire roof “protected.” S1, S2, C1 and E1 cover these questions.

5. Wind: distinguish ventilation, shelter and clean air

Previous notes discuss SW/WSW and NNW seasonal winds. They justify testing alternatives, not claiming an observed site wind rose. NASA POWER meteorological values come from gridded assimilation/model products; requesting a coordinate does not turn the result into an on-plot sensor record. NASA POWER data FAQ.

The new map therefore has SW 225°, WSW 257°, NNW 337° and calm/variable comparison settings. The arrow starts on the side the wind is from and points toward the plot. It has no speed, frequency, plume width or reach meaning. These cases are not a complete climatic distribution and do not assert the direction during a particular season or hour.

Design implication: test how a selective edge, offset opening and roof configuration behave under more than one direction. A fully enclosed court may weaken useful exchange; a large opening may expose occupied space to unwanted wind or incoming polluted air. Neither outcome is calculated by the drawn arrows. Compare calm hot conditions as well as useful breezes, and avoid placing a permanent “clean-air entrance” based only on a regional direction.

The river corridor and sea-facing context remain spatial facts, not proof of cooling. Local wind can differ between street, court and roof. Obtain a documented weather series, directional frequencies and field observations before evaluating any airflow model. A CFD image with assumed inputs would remain a scenario, not field validation. Map W1, W2, C1 and E1 distinguish those levels of evidence.

6. Rain, drainage and ground: three different water questions

First, rain falling on the project needs collection, weather protection and a safe overflow concept. Second, street/plot runoff and drainage backflow depend on actual levels, inlets and receiving systems. Third, river or wider flood mechanisms require appropriate hydraulic information. The blue river polygon proves none of their extents or probabilities.

Nearby district work demonstrates that drainage is an operational urban issue: UN-Habitat describes drainage and lighting improvements in Maraach. That is not evidence of a flood at our plot or a capacity assessment of Street 80. UN-Habitat, 2022 intervention account.

Design implication: draw the entrance, shelter, surface levels and possible overflow together. A raised dry platform may become an access barrier; a covered entrance may discharge water onto the route below. Neither should be fixed without measured levels. No flow arrows are currently added to the plan because the slope and connections are unknown. Observe rain from safe accessible locations; do not enter drains, floodwater or restricted infrastructure.

Ground is a separate dependency. Infiltration can interact with groundwater and contaminants; it is not automatically appropriate on every urban site. Prior land use should guide investigation, but vacancy or industrial context does not by itself prove contamination. US EPA groundwater protection, US EPA urban agriculture FAQ.

Soil profile, bearing capacity, groundwater, contamination, infiltration and relevant water quality remain TBD. Keep options for controlled growing media, lined storage or approved drainage until the ground investigation is available. Hydroponics avoids some soil contact but does not certify its water, air, structure or operations. The roof-growing idea needs a service and maintenance plan as well as a sunlight diagram. R1, R2, G1 and E1 locate these questions without inventing conditions.

7. Traffic and visual exposure: shelter without exclusion

Protection also concerns the space between arrival and entry. Street 80 is source-mapped as a one-way tertiary road; this is not a measured vehicle speed, clear pavement width or approved driveway. The mapped bridge and steps establish an arrival component, not a universally usable route. Street 80 source, whole-journey accessibility analysis.

Design implication: give waiting, walking and loading distinct operational space; keep the entrance easy to recognise and reach. Do not place a wall, tree pit or canopy support where it narrows the approach. An inner layer can be quiet without making the public entry hidden, and separate after-hours controls can protect inner uses without closing every shared space at once.

Visual exposure also changes with height. Bridge users and neighbours may see over a low enclosure; those sightlines need verified levels and viewpoints. Privacy should be tested for the activity and room, not used as a claim that the area is unsafe. No crime, danger or night-safety classification is inferred from a road or photograph. Evening legibility, glare and spill toward neighbours remain field/design questions. T1, T2, V1 and V2 connect these issues back to accessibility and daily operation.

8. How this develops nesting into design decisions

The conclusion is not “the plot is exposed, therefore surround it with a wall.” It is:

Different pressures meet at this small urban interface, but they do not have the same direction, height or timing. The project should create a sequence of more controlled spaces, with each layer doing a specific job and with openings chosen through testing. That is why nesting is a useful design hypothesis here.

The first layer should be an accessible sheltered threshold, not a barrier. The next can selectively separate road-facing activity and servicing from longer stays. A shaded shared space can then be compared under several opening arrangements. Inner rooms can have more controlled sound, privacy and ventilation. The roof remains a separate environmental layer. These are relationships to design, not a fixed programme, ownership boundary or guaranteed performance.

Comparison What it might help What could work against it Evidence needed to choose
A road-side buffer with an offset entrance Separate long stays from the traffic interface Open ends, upper sound exposure, airflow restriction, hidden entry Paired sections, acoustic assessment, wind/air cases, arrival audit
A more open shaded court Shade and exchange with the surroundings Sound ingress, incoming emissions, low-angle sun, wind discomfort Corrected heights, seasonal sun, source/wind observations
A partly enclosed shared space with independently controlled quiet rooms Different conditions and hours for different activities System/maintenance needs, glare, re-entry of exhaust, operational complexity Programme/time brief, ventilation strategy, service locations

These alternatives should use the same assumed programme and observation points so that the comparison is fair. Do not choose a winner because its diagram looks most protective. The choice should identify which benefits are supported, which are hypotheses and which costs/operational demands are accepted.

10. Field and modelling plan

Start with a common reference plan and repeatable observation positions: lawful accessible road-side locations, available plot interfaces, the proposed arrival and representative interior/upper-level positions only where access is authorised. The map’s tags suggest questions, not permission to enter property or stand beside moving traffic. Record date, weather, location, method and any uncertainty with every observation.

Priority Collect Why it changes design Completion condition
1 — Levels and geometry Plot/road/landing levels; neighbouring and bridge heights; verified footprint references Sound, sightlines, shade, drainage and access all depend on this A coordinated plan and two sections with uncertainty marked
2 — Sound and activity Paired edge/inner observations at comparable periods, event notes, appropriate calibrated sound measurements Tests quiet-space and opening choices without mixing different traffic conditions Method, intervals, instrument/calibration and weather documented; no extrapolated night result
3 — Air sources and weather Actual outlets/idling/dust activities, source heights and operating information; suitable monitoring and local wind Tests intake, servicing and court alternatives Verified source inventory and qualified interpretation, not guessed emission rates
4 — Solar and heat Verified heights/canopies; occupied-hour shade observations; suitable surface/comfort assessment Chooses screen/canopy/tree roles and roof conditions Seasonal geometry checked separately from thermal performance
5 — Wet ground and growing Drain/invert/outfall levels, safe rain observations, ground investigation, relevant soil/water testing Establishes threshold, drainage and growing feasibility Specialist advice and applicable requirements before sizing or claiming safety
6 — Operation and people Evening/wet arrival, loading, queues, legibility, resting and privacy needs Keeps protection inclusive and maintainable Findings linked to the Accessibility and Daily Rhythm studies

Then compare the three nesting arrangements above using consistent inputs. Measurements can refine a model; a model does not erase uncertainty in those measurements. Specialist acoustic, environmental, drainage, geotechnical, structural and local compliance advice will be needed before technical guarantees or construction decisions. This research does not specify a certified barrier, foundation, flood defence or ventilation system.

11. Corrections and unresolved evidence

Earlier project notes contain strong claims derived from an older drawing and regional data. Their historical record is retained, but they must not be used as settled evidence for this corrected plot:

  • Old percentages of the plot within road-distance bands are not a noise/air measurement or a finding that every point lacks shelter.
  • Statements that a northern neighbour can “never” shade the site, or that no neighbour can shade without a particular storey count, need new geometry, heights and seasonal testing. They are not repeated as findings here.
  • Regional SW/WSW/NNW descriptions are not a site wind rose; an annual average direction does not provide directional frequencies or comfort at ground level.
  • Conflicting rainfall totals and differing source periods/resolutions remain VERIFY. No figure has been selected by averaging or by assuming that one is an underestimate.
  • River engineering descriptions, regional hazard screening and reported groundwater values do not provide our site flood level, design rainfall, safe floor elevation or water table.
  • Vegetation and industrial context certify neither safe growing ground nor contamination.
  • The cited 2021 Beirut generator preprint is Al Aawar et al., correcting the author attribution in older notes. It is not our plot’s monitoring record.

The six investigations are therefore desk-analysis complete / site-performance verification incomplete. There is enough evidence to form and compare design questions, but not enough to declare the plot unprotected in every sense or a proposed nest environmentally successful.
