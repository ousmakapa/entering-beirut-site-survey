Daily Rhythm — How the Area Changes Throughout the Day

Working conclusion

Our site should be understood as a place where different daily schedules overlap, not as a district that is busy by day and empty at night. The useful design question is which parts of a mixed-use hub should be accessible together, which should close separately, and which need protection while neighbouring activities continue.

The current evidence supports investigating this overlap. It does not establish a measured hourly activity pattern at our plot. Published local-business hours differ, nearby uses are mixed, and historical neighbourhood research records changing street occupation. The most promising period to investigate is the afternoon/evening handover: some work may continue while other people return home, shop or seek learning and meeting space. This is a design hypothesis, not a proven peak or a confirmed audience.

Study scope: the existing detailed map around the Beirut River, highway, plot-side street and adjacent northern/southern blocks. “Upper” means north, “lower” means south; neither means a different elevation or social class. Research checked 2026-09-14. Current operator schedules remain VERIFY; exact-site repeated observations are TBD.

What the new map shows

Open Daily Rhythm, following Connections and People & Needs. There are six clock-time bands: 05–08, 08–12, 12–15, 15–18, 18–21 and 21–05. These are analytical sampling periods, not observed peaks, shift boundaries or sunrise/sunset times. Afternoon is the initial view because it exposes the handover question most clearly.

Four dashed study windows and twelve clickable tags link locations to time-specific questions, evidence, design consequences and verification tasks. Window colours represent the type of question: work, home life, movement, overlap, rest/lighting, or unknown. Constant pale opacity does not encode intensity. No building is painted “open” based on its recorded use. Source roads and bridge components remain geometrically unchanged through the day; their actual operating conditions are not known. Tag leader lines only connect displaced labels to analytical points.

The day-type selector distinguishes ordinary Monday/Tuesday/Thursday/Friday, Wednesday, Saturday and Sunday. Only the two explicit operator examples change their advertised hours. The local scenario shading does not pretend to have measured weekend differences. A separate Forum toggle adds one dated advertised event comparison; it does not assert an event on the selected generic day. Evidence only hides all local time hypotheses and keeps source geometry, Forum context and the published-hours panel. Off-map examples are not assigned to invented footprints.

Evidence that changes the argument

1. Different businesses have different clocks

Crochet Saro publishes 09:30–19:00, Monday–Saturday, for its two Arax Street shops, with Sunday closure. Unigaz’s Shell Street/Seaside Road storefront publishes 08:30–20:40 on most Monday–Saturday days, an earlier Wednesday finish at 14:00, and Sunday 12:00–20:40. These are individual operator statements, not a representative sample or confirmed current operation. They disprove the shortcut of assigning one universal “working day” to every business. Neither exact shop footprint is assigned in this map. Crochet Saro, Unigaz

A third operator page illustrates uncertainty: Jolibelle’s main hours show weekday closing at 19:00 and Saturday at 19:00; its footer says 20:00 and 18:00 respectively. Both statements are preserved here; neither is selected as correct. The shop is excluded from the comparison until confirmed. Jolibelle

Site inference: do not draw a whole northern zone shutting at 17:00, or a whole neighbourhood closing on Sunday. Establish which actual doors, services and routes are available at the hour a proposed user would arrive. An advertised open window still does not show customer numbers, worker breaks, deliveries or willingness to visit our project.

2. Street space can change function without changing shape

Dagher and Samaha’s October 2016 fieldwork on Sis Street in Nabaa, a different Bourj Hammoud subarea, describes shop spill-out, deliveries throughout the day despite an early-morning bias, vendors’ daytime positions and night storage, and late shops contributing light and activity. See printed pp.10,13,15. These are historical observations, not today’s conditions beside our plot; the paper’s later design proposals must not be mistaken for existing arrangements. AUB working paper

Site inference: on Street 80, the relevant question is not just “is this a road?” but “what occupies its edge when someone arrives?” A mapped connection can be obstructed by loading for several minutes without disappearing from the plan. The temporal analysis therefore needs obstruction duration and remaining walking space, not another static road arrow.

3. Workshop closure and residential quiet are not the same event

The Public Source’s 27 March 2024 firsthand report includes residents above or facing workshops between Dawra and Bourj Hammoud describing waiting for work to finish, sometimes into the evening. Its wider reporting also identifies traffic and generators as continuing sources. Photographs are dated July 2022. These accounts do not supply measured sound at our plot; the Yerevan and Mar Mikhael sections must not be geographically transferred to our highway edge. The Public Source

Site inference: the sound mix may change even when it does not become quiet. A workshop tool stopping could leave highway noise more noticeable; that possibility requires listening or measurement. Homes above commercial ground floors also introduce a different receiver at a different height. A late hub event could add a new disturbance during someone else’s rest period. No decibel values, reduction percentages or quiet-zone contours are justified yet.

4. Evening access depends on the route, not just the destination

UN-Habitat’s November 2022 account describes rehabilitating five alleyways in Maraach and a resident reporting greater confidence letting children play after solar lighting was installed. This is evidence of an intervention elsewhere, not evidence that our bridge is illuminated or that lighting alone establishes safety. UN-Habitat

Site inference: recheck C1’s two landings, the street transition and the plot entrance after dark. A bright lobby cannot compensate for every unverified part of the approach. Also distinguish municipal lamps, shop light, private projectors and the proposed project’s lights: they may have different operating or power schedules. Current local lighting and outage behaviour are TBD.

5. Events produce exceptional windows, not everyday demand

Forum’s own website advertises the Royal Wedding Fair for 28 October–1 November 2026, 17:00–23:00. As of the research date this is a future listing, not an observed event. That advertised window is the map’s event comparison; no attendance, departures after closing or spillover to our plot is inferred. Forum de Beyrouth

An ISF notice dated 27 June 2026 described traffic measures for 28 June’s activities, including the 07:00–11:00 cycling event and restrictions on the road from Forum Square toward Emile Lahoud. This is a dated exception, not a normal Sunday closure or proof that our footbridge was closed. ISF notice

Site inference: compare an ordinary day with a specific event day. Check both outward and return trips. A larger crowd west of the river does not automatically become project users east of it, and transport arrangements may themselves change during an event.

6. Learning follows programme and season, not a universal hour

YFC Lebanon’s Manara Youth Center describes after-school support and summer activities in Bourj Hammoud. This confirms a provider’s different programme types, not an exact school bell time, neutral district-wide demand, or a route from a school to our plot. No precise hours are adopted. Provider description

Site inference: a learning room may be most useful when potential users are available, not when offices are most active. Test this with providers and users before extending the hub’s hours. Existing programmes also mean a new offer should complement, not simply duplicate, them.

Six periods — what to test and what it could change

The following matrix is authored interpretation grounded in the mixed-use and transport context above. It is not an observed timetable. Time differences are questions until verified.

The night band crosses midnight and is deliberately broad. A late-evening sample cannot describe the whole band. Split it if later evidence justifies doing so. Saturday and Sunday need separate observation; residential presence must not be converted into a fabricated weekend street crowd. Religious holidays, school holidays, weather and exceptional events should be logged as conditions, not represented as stereotyped behaviour of particular communities.

The less visible relationships worth investigating

  1. Duration: ten people walking past and ten people staying for half an hour require different support. Count movement separately from dwell time; neither can currently be inferred.
  2. Vertical overlap: a working ground floor and a resting or studying upper floor may coexist. The analysis therefore needs a section as well as a plan; storey labels alone do not establish who occupies each level or which windows are open.
  3. The handover gap: offices closing before a later session may leave an unstaffed interval. Test who controls the entrance, toilets, cleaning and security during that interval.
  4. The reversible edge: a loading stop, parked vehicle or shop display changes usable access without changing the street polygon. Record minutes and clear space, not just locations.
  5. The return journey: a route used in daylight may be experienced differently after a session. Observe the whole safe public approach; avoid labelling it safe or unsafe without evidence.
  6. The environmental clocks: sun/shade, local business activity, highway sound and mechanical services need not peak together. A quieter period is not automatically cooler or cleaner.
  7. Season: 18:00 is not always after dark. Use dated shadow/daylight studies separately; this map does not calculate sun position, shadows, air quality, thermal comfort or lighting levels.
  8. Operating cost: keeping a small shared room open still requires staff, facilities and power. “Flexible” is credible only if the room can actually be operated independently.

From temporal analysis to design decisions

The official programme already contains public/commercial, exhibition/cultural, business/work, learning/workshop, ecological/productive and service functions. This study does not change those allocations or authorise a school, factory, night shelter or 24-hour entertainment venue.

Proposed operating logic, for testing:

  • Arrival layer: identifiable welcome and sheltered waiting close to the eventual entrance. It should not force a visitor through controlled work areas or a café purchase.
  • Shared layer: learning, meeting and display spaces that could serve a limited evening programme when offices are closed. Give them access to the support facilities they actually need.
  • Quiet layer: work and concentration protected from demonstrations, road exposure and delivery noise. It can close separately without severing the shared layer’s exit route.
  • Service layer: deliveries, refuse, maintenance and productive systems managed without occupying public waiting. Plant may need to run when public rooms are closed; its acoustic and maintenance requirements must be investigated independently.

This gives nesting an operational test: can different layers remain connected when useful but be controlled and protected when their schedules diverge? It does not imply a street inside the building or a fixed concentric form. A plan or section that needs every floor unlocked for a small evening session would fail this particular test. Likewise, a protective shell that hides the entrance or makes the arrival uncomfortable would undermine the public objective.

The strongest proposition to discuss with the professor is:

The surrounding uses do not share one timetable. I am testing a hub that can open in layers: a reachable shared part can serve later activities while quiet work and servicing remain separately controlled. The exact hours and audience still need local verification.

This is a defensible design direction because it responds to programme diversity and mixed surroundings. It is not proof that any particular evening function will be successful.

Focused field plan — the remaining evidence

Start with five repeat stations: T2 immediate working frontage, T5 a safe highway receiver, T6 bridge landing context, T7 plot-side lane and T8 cross-river approach. Tags are analytical locations, not instructions to stand in a roadway. Choose safe public observation positions first.

At each station, record a 10-minute sample with exact start/end time. Repeat in relevant periods on an ordinary weekday and a weekend day. Use rotating visit order or separate visits: one person cannot observe all five simultaneously. Treat the first samples as a pilot, not a representative average. Add repeat days if patterns differ or events/weather make them atypical.

These cannot be closed by recolouring existing photographs.

What belongs on the eventual board

Board artwork remains deferred. When evidence improves, the clearest content would be:

  • A small set of same-extent plans comparing selected periods, with an explicit confidence key.
  • A time strip separating recorded business windows, observed activity samples and proposed project operating modes—never one combined unlabeled heatmap.
  • One street elevation or section showing ground-floor work, upper-floor sensitivity and the highway/entrance relationship, once heights and uses are checked.
  • A simple axonometric operating diagram showing which project layers open/close independently.
  • Only dated, matched-view photo pairs where they establish an actual temporal change.

The current map is the working evidence-and-hypothesis tool for that discussion, not a finished board and not a completed temporal field survey.
